# Writing for the MIT

## Purpose

The wiki supports the business model and the business plan. It educates,
explains and illustrates the concepts that fall out of them.

A model tells you the contribution per bird. The wiki tells you what
contribution is, why it is not the same as profit, and what has to happen
before that number can be trusted. Someone should be able to read the
model with the wiki open beside it and follow every move.

James decides which articles exist. Do not audit the library or argue a
page out of existence. Each article still needs a single subject, and
anything that belongs to a second subject belongs on a second page.

## Voice

Teach a twelfth grader the same information. Clear, unhurried and exact,
with no padding and no simplification that costs accuracy. Full
sentences, real vocabulary, and a term defined the first time it appears.

Assume competence. The reader knows what a chicken and an invoice are, so
explaining the obvious wastes their time and makes the article longer
than the subject deserves. Explain the concepts this model invents, and
leave the rest alone.

The voice stays neutral throughout. The wiki explains what a thing is and
how it works. It does not sell the plan.

## Shape of a page

1. **Title.**
2. **Lead section.** One or two paragraphs, before the first heading,
   that say what the subject is. Bold the subject the first time it
   appears. A reader who stops at the end of the lead should understand
   the subject and why it exists.
3. **Body**, under headings.
4. **Next and previous**, linking the articles either side of this one in
   reading order.

**Never open with a section describing the page.** *Why this page
exists*, *what this covers*, *what you will find below*: throat-clearing,
and it reproduces itself onto the next page written. Where a page
genuinely needs context first, that context becomes its own page and the
content links to it in one line at the top.

**Headings are labels, not sentences.** `Feed cost`, not `Why the feed
cost figure is probably wrong`. Under thirty characters, forty is the
ceiling, no leading articles, no trailing punctuation, no questions.

## Topic sentences

Every body paragraph opens with a topic sentence. It is the most
important sentence in the paragraph and it states the main idea.

- **Placement.** First sentence of the paragraph.
- **Two parts.** The topic, which is the broad subject, and a
  controlling idea, which is the specific claim the paragraph makes
  about it.
- **Function.** A signpost. It previews the point before any evidence or
  example arrives, so the reader is never assembling meaning backward.

A paragraph that cannot be summarized by its first sentence is a
paragraph doing two jobs. Split it.

## Sentences

- Write complete sentences. No fragments.
- No comma splices. Two independent clauses need a period or a
  conjunction.
- Do not punctuate sentences with dashes or semicolons. Use a period, a
  comma, or a colon.

## Banned outright

Treat every violation as a defect rather than a preference.

1. **British English.** Any `-our` for `-or`, `-ise` for `-ize`, `-ence`
   for `-ense`, doubled `-lled`/`-lling`, `whilst`, `amongst`. The rule
   describes patterns rather than listing words on purpose: an earlier
   version listed them and a spelling sweep rewrote the list itself.
2. **Em dashes.** Use periods, commas, colons or a line break.
   Quotations are exempt and keep their punctuation verbatim.
3. **The word "spine"** for an ordered list or a timeline.
4. **A fragment after another fragment.** One was dramatic enough.
5. **A sentence split into a sentence plus a fragment** where the
   fragment only adds emphasis. Fold it back in.
6. **Reversal pivots.** *It's not X, it's Y. Not X but Y. The real story
   is Y.*
7. **Filler transitions.** *At its core. In today's world. That said.
   Ultimately. What this means is. It's important to note.*
8. **Therapy language.** *I hear you. That sounds hard.*
9. **Meta commentary.** *In this section. We will discuss. Key
   takeaways.*
10. **Symmetry padding.** Balancing clauses that exist only for balance,
    and three-part lists that were not earned.

## Required

- Declarative sentences, neutral tone and voice, vary length and word
  choice.
- Concrete nouns over abstractions, prefer gentle and matter-of-fact to
  strong and attention-grabbing.
- No hedging unless the uncertainty is real, and then state it plainly.
- Close without summary. Never restate the thesis.

## Mechanics

- No trailing whitespace.
- For bold and italics, follow the Wikipedia rules below.

## Text formatting

These rules follow the Wikipedia Manual of Style, [Text formatting][mos].

**Bold** is for the subject of the article at its first appearance in
the lead, and for terms that redirect to it. Bold is not for emphasis in
running text, and not for introducing a new term.

*Italics* are for emphasis, for the titles of major works, for a word
being discussed as a word, and for non-English terms that have not been
absorbed into English. Naturalized loanwords stay upright.

Do not italicize a quotation. Quotation marks or a block quote are
sufficient on their own. Italics inside a quotation are reserved for
emphasis that was in the source, or for non-English words already there.
Where emphasis is added, mark it.

Never use all caps, small caps, or underlining for emphasis. Underlining
reads as a link. Prose text is never colored.

This document is the one exception, and it declares it here. A rules file
is read by somebody scanning for the rule they need, so each rule opens
with its name in bold. Articles do not get that licence.

[mos]: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Text_formatting

## Structure over prose

Reach for a structure before another paragraph. Unordered lists, tables,
diagrams, charts and graphs all carry relationships that prose has to
spell out slowly. A comparison across three options is a table. A
sequence of stages is a diagram. A distribution is a chart.

Prose explains what the structure means. It does not repeat what the
structure already shows.

## Numbers

**Never type a number into a sentence.** A figure appears as a citation
to the register, carrying its confidence mark, or it does not appear.
Writing *"3,000 birds clears the goal"* creates a sentence that silently
becomes false the moment feed price moves, sitting directly above the
figure that falsifies it.

This extends to **threshold verbs**. *Clears*, *falls short*, *covers*,
*is enough* are all numeric claims wearing words. Either the verb comes
from the register too, or the sentence is rewritten so it does not
assert. When a passage cannot survive without stating a value, that
passage belongs in the model where the value is live.

**Typed, not computed.** A generated page may interpolate a figure into
a sentence, because it is recalculated on every build and cannot fall out
of step with the data behind it. The rule is against numbers written by
hand into text that nothing recomputes.

Do not put placeholders into the text. Where a figure does not exist
yet, leave the sentence out and ask.

Where a number has changed, link its lineage rather than retelling the
history.

## People

**No personal data enters this repo unless James says to add it.** Not
mortgage or property debt, not will material, not the wage split, not
descriptions of health or character. Where a page needs it, leave a
named gap and ask.

Two habits read as advocacy and are banned:

- **Praising the material.** *The sharpest idea. The most important
  finding.* Rank by evidence, not by adjective.
- **Bundling.** Stacking loosely related facts so a conclusion feels
  inevitable. Separate them and let each stand.

## Maintaining

**Delete out-of-date, stale, or superseded content.** Keep the library
as tidy as you can.

**When a figure moves, the articles citing it do not need editing.**
That is the whole point of citing the register. If you find yourself
editing prose because a number changed, the prose was breaking the
numbers rule.

**Check any page you touch.** Does its explanation still match the
current model? Does every link resolve? Is the next and previous
navigation still correct?

## Before you commit

1. **Run the `proofreader` agent. This is mandatory, not advisory.** It
   runs over every prose file the change touches, including generated
   articles and the strings that produce them. A hand check does not
   substitute for it, and neither does confidence in the draft.
   The agent holds no rules of its own. It reads this file, so the two
   cannot drift, and a rule changed here takes effect on the next run.
2. Apply the findings, or say which you are rejecting and why. A finding
   that survives is a defect that ships.
3. **Show James the rendered prose and wait.** All written content needs
   his approval before it is committed. Generate it, show it, stop.
